Invariance tables

Reads the Mplus output files of a measurement invariance sequence with two or more groups, places every model on one step of a fixed ladder using the equality constraints printed in TECH1, compares only models that sit on adjacent steps, and reports the fit table, the difference tests, the modification indices and the behavior of each item across groups.

What to request from Mplus. OUTPUT: TECH1 MODINDICES STDYX SAMPSTAT; in every model of the sequence. TECH1 is the part that decides everything else: it prints, group by group, the number Mplus assigns to each free parameter, and the same number in every group is what identifies a parameter held equal across groups. Without TECH1 a model is still listed and its fit is still read, but it enters no comparison, because degrees of freedom alone do not establish that one constraint set contains another. MODINDICES fills the modification-index table and STDYX SAMPSTAT the item plot. With the abbreviated syntax MODEL = CONFIGURAL METRIC SCALAR; ask only for TECH1, which is all Mplus accepts there; one such file holds the whole sequence and the page splits it into its models.

Load the output files

Drop the .out files of the sequence into the box, or press it to choose them; several files at once is the normal case. Each file is read by your browser and is not transmitted anywhere.

The step of each model is read from the constraints in TECH1. The name of the file and the title of the run are used only when TECH1 is missing, and then only as a hint. You can override the step in the list below; the page checks the override against TECH1 and reports a conflict when the two disagree.

Two or more groups. Reading the constraints, the chi-square contribution of each group, the difference tests and the item plot are all defined for k groups. The one part that is not general is the reading of a step from a file name, which recognizes the usual English names of the steps and nothing else.

    How to read the changes in fit

    The difference test answers whether the added constraints worsen fit beyond sampling error. With a large sample it reaches significance for a difference of no practical size, which is why the change in the approximate fit indices is reported next to it. The values below have been proposed as guidance, each under the conditions its authors studied. A change exactly equal to a cutoff counts here as within it.

    • Cheung and Rensvold (2002): a change in CFI of at least −.010, that is a drop no larger than .010, supports the constrained model. Their simulations used two groups and normal continuous indicators.
    • Chen (2007): for groups of comparable size with more than 300 cases in each, a change in CFI of −.010 together with a change in RMSEA of .015 and a change in SRMR of .030 when the constraints are on loadings, or .010 when they are on intercepts, thresholds or residual variances. For unequal groups with 300 or fewer cases in each, the stricter values are −.005 for CFI, .010 for RMSEA and .025 for SRMR with loadings or .005 with intercepts and residual variances. Those are the two designs she studied; a sequence that falls between them, equal groups of 200 for instance, matches neither, and the comparison table then lists both sets and marks neither as matching.
    • Rutkowski and Svetina (2014), for comparisons across many groups: a change in CFI of −.020 with a change in RMSEA of .030 at the metric step, and −.010 with .010 at the scalar step. Their design used 10 and 20 groups of 600 to 6,000 cases each, with one continuous factor, and the page marks their values as matching only a sequence with at least ten groups.
    • Meade, Johnson and Braddy (2008) found ΔCFI to be affected little by sample size and by the number of indicators and factors, which is why one value serves for the whole design, and proposed −.002 as a criterion more sensitive than −.010; it flags differences that the larger cutoffs let through. Their cutoffs for ΔMcDonald's NCI, which the page does not compute, do depend on the number of factors and indicators.
    • Svetina, Rutkowski and Rutkowski (2020) set out the sequence for ordered-categorical indicators, where thresholds enter alongside loadings, and collect the cutoffs proposed for that case. They state that the values are not interchangeable across designs and that the ones derived for continuous indicators should not be carried over to categorical ones without checking.

    These criteria are approximate. They come from simulations run under specific conditions of sample size, number and size of groups, number of indicators, type of indicator and kind and amount of misspecification, and they do not exhaust the evaluation. That evaluation also rests on the theory behind the instrument, on the size and the location of the non-invariant parameters, on what those parameters do to the comparisons the study is about, and on the decision, which is the researcher's, to accept partial invariance and to report which constraints were released and why. A change within a cutoff is not evidence that the constraints hold, and a change beyond one is not by itself a reason to reject them. Putnick and Bornstein (2016) and Vandenberg and Lance (2000) review how the sequence is reported and where it goes wrong; Millsap (2011) treats what invariance does and does not license about the comparison of groups.

    Modification indices

    The behavior of an item across groups

    The figure draws, for one item, what the model says its expected value is at each score on the factor, one line per group. Two lines with different slopes are a loading that differs; two parallel lines at different heights are an intercept or a threshold that differs.

    Export settings

    Both figures use these settings.

    Journals ask for 8 to 10 pt at the printed width; the figure is drawn at the size it will be printed. Colors are from the Okabe-Ito palette and every group also has its own dash pattern, so the figure survives grayscale printing; the marker shapes are a third cue.

    One parameter, every item, every group

    The second figure puts one class of parameters of the selected model on a single axis: every item on the vertical axis, the estimate in each group with its 95% confidence interval on the horizontal one. An item whose parameter moves between groups is the row whose points do not line up. Rows the model holds equal across groups carry one estimate and are marked as such, so that a constraint is never mistaken for an empirical agreement.

    The model is the one chosen for the first figure, and so are the type size, the width and the resolution.

    Methods and formulas

    The ladder and where a model sits on it

    Every model that the page reads is placed on exactly one step of a fixed ladder. The steps, in order, are: configural, no parameter held equal across groups; metric, the loadings held equal; scalar, the loadings and the intercepts, or the thresholds when the indicators are ordered-categorical; strict, also the residual variances; then two optional structural steps, the factor variances and covariances, and the factor means. Nothing else is a step. A model that releases part of the constraints of its step, such as a scalar model with one intercept left free, sits on that same step and carries the flag partial. Partial invariance is a property of a model at a step, not a step of its own; treating it as a step is what lets two models be compared that stand in no nesting relation.

    The step comes from the constraints Mplus actually imposed, read in TECH1. Mplus numbers the free parameters group by group and prints the numbers matrix by matrix; a parameter that carries the same number in every group is estimated once for all groups, which is what an equality constraint across groups means, and a parameter printed as 0 is not free. The page collects, for each class of parameters, how many cells are equal in every group, how many are free, and how many are equal in some groups but not all. A class is fully constrained when no cell of it is left loose, partial when some are, and free when none is constrained. The step is then the highest class that is constrained at all:

    factor means constrained          -> step 5, factor means
factor variances and covariances  -> step 4, factor variances and covariances
residual variances constrained    -> step 3, strict
intercepts or thresholds          -> step 2, scalar
loadings                          -> step 1, metric
nothing                           -> step 0, configural

    Factor means, factor variances and, with ordered-categorical indicators, the scale factors or the residual variances of the underlying response variables are fixed at the start of the sequence to identify the model, not to impose invariance. The page counts them as constraints only once the loadings and the intercepts or thresholds are themselves constrained. Counting them earlier would make the configural model look more constrained than the scalar one and the whole ladder would run backwards.

    The name of the file and the title of the run are read only when TECH1 is absent, and then only as a hint, which the page says. When you set the step yourself, the page compares your choice with the step the constraints imply and reports the disagreement instead of hiding it.

    Which models are compared

    A comparison is produced only between two models on adjacent steps, and only in these forms: the full model of a step against the full model of the next step, the full model of a step against the partial model of the next step, and, when a full and a partial model share one step, the two against each other, which is the test of the constraints the partial model releases. No other pair is compared. In particular two models on steps that are not adjacent are never compared, even when their degrees of freedom would allow it.

    Adjacency alone is not enough. Before any test the page checks the constraint sets themselves. Each constrained cell is written as a set of atoms: one atom for every pair of groups that share a parameter number, and one atom for every group in which the parameter is not free, for the classes where a fixed parameter is an invariance constraint rather than an identifying restriction. The comparison is produced only when the set of one model is a proper superset of the set of the other; the model with the larger set is the restricted one, the other is the unrestricted one, and the page names both together with the number of restrictions that separates them, class by class. When the sets are equal, or when neither contains the other, no test is produced and the page says which constraints are present in one model and missing in the other. Since the number of restrictions counted this way is the difference in free parameters, the page also checks it against the difference in degrees of freedom and reports a mismatch.

    The ladder has one exception, and it is stated wherever it applies. With ordered-categorical indicators the thresholds and the loadings are not separately identified in the usual parameterization, so the metric step is not a step of its own and the sequence goes from the configural model to one that constrains both (Wu & Estabrook, 2016). When every model read carries thresholds and none of them constrains the loadings alone, the page marks the metric step as not applicable, closes the ladder over it, and compares the scalar step with the configural one, saying so in the row. If a model that constrains the loadings alone is loaded, the step is occupied and the ordinary adjacency applies again.

    Without TECH1 there is no constraint set, so the model takes part in no comparison. Degrees of freedom are a necessary condition and not a sufficient one: two models can differ by three degrees of freedom without either one being a restriction of the other, and a difference test computed on such a pair looks entirely plausible and means nothing.

    Groups

    Everything above is defined for k groups with k at least 2: a cell is constrained when it carries the same parameter number in every one of the k groups, and the count of restrictions on a cell is the number of parameters it removes, that is the number of free groups minus the number of distinct parameter numbers among them. A cell that is equal in some groups and free in others is reported separately and is not treated as a constraint of the class, because the class is then neither constrained nor free. The chi-square contribution of each group is read for all k groups. The item plot draws one line per group. The one part that is not general is the reading of the sequence from a file name, which recognizes the usual English names of the steps only.

    The difference test, by estimator

    The test depends on the estimator, and the page never assumes one: when the estimator cannot be read it asks for it in the model list and computes nothing until it is given.

    ML, MLF, GLS and WLS produce a statistic that is asymptotically chi-square distributed, and the difference of two of them is again chi-square distributed:

    T  = chi2_restricted - chi2_unrestricted
df = df_restricted - df_unrestricted

    MLR, MLM and WLSM print a statistic already divided by a scaling correction factor c, so the scaled difference test of Satorra and Bentler (2001) multiplies the printed values back before differencing them:

    cd = (df_0 * c_0 - df_1 * c_1) / (df_0 - df_1)
T  = (chi2_0 * c_0 - chi2_1 * c_1) / cd

    with the index 0 for the restricted model and 1 for the unrestricted one, and T referred to a chi-square distribution with df_0 − df_1 degrees of freedom. When cd comes out zero or negative, or when T comes out negative, the test is not a non-significant test: the statistic in that form cannot be referred to a chi-square distribution and nothing follows from it, for either model. Satorra and Bentler (2010) give a version that is positive by construction. It needs one further Mplus run of the restricted model in which the unrestricted solution supplies the starting values and estimation stops before the first update, ANALYSIS: ITERATIONS = 0;, whose scaling correction factor c10 replaces c_0 in the numerator of cd. That run is a separate file and is not part of an invariance sequence, so the page reports the obstruction and points to the model comparison tool, which takes the third output.

    MLMV, WLSMV and ULSMV adjust both the mean and the variance of the statistic, and the difference of two such statistics is not chi-square distributed under any rescaling. The test has to be produced by Mplus with the DIFFTEST option, in two runs:

    ! Step 1: the less restricted model
SAVEDATA:   DIFFTEST = deriv.dat;

! Step 2: the more restricted model, same data and variables
ANALYSIS:   DIFFTEST = deriv.dat;

    The second output then carries a block headed Chi-Square Test for Difference Testing. The page reads that block from the restricted model of each pair and reports it; when the block is absent it prints the two runs to make and computes nothing. ULS prints no chi-square test of model fit, and BAYES prints none either, so for those two estimators there is no difference test to compute.

    The page recomputes every test from the fit statistics of the two models. When the sequence comes from the abbreviated syntax MODEL = CONFIGURAL METRIC SCALAR;, Mplus prints difference tests of its own inside that file; the page does not use them. It splits the file into its models, places each on the ladder from its own TECH1 and computes the tests itself, so that the pair being tested is stated and can be checked.

    Differences in fit indices

    Each difference is the value of the restricted model minus the value of the unrestricted model, for CFI, RMSEA and SRMR, so that a drop in CFI is negative and a rise in RMSEA is positive. The cutoffs proposed for those differences are collected in the guidance section above, with the conditions each was derived under; the comparison table marks a difference as within or beyond the criteria that match this comparison, by a label and a glyph and not by color alone.

    Modification indices

    The modification index of a constraint is the drop in the chi-square that releasing that one constraint would produce, tested on one degree of freedom, and the expected parameter change is the value the parameter would take. The thresholds offered are the .05, .01 and .001 quantiles of a chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom, 3.841, 6.635 and 10.828. The indices are shown per model and per group and sorted by size. Under an equality constraint across groups the same index appears in every group, with expected changes of opposite sign: it is one constraint, not k of them, and releasing it costs one degree of freedom in total. A high index says where to look, not what to conclude; constraints are released one at a time, the model is refitted, and the number of released constraints belongs in the report.

    The two figures

    The first figure draws one item across the groups. For a continuous indicator the expected value of item j in group g is a straight line in the factor:

    E(y_j | eta) = nu_jg + lambda_jg * eta

    drawn over the range each group covers, from its own factor mean minus 2.5 factor standard deviations to its mean plus 2.5, with the band option adding nu + lambda*eta ± k*sqrt(theta_jg) for k equal to 1 or 1.96, where theta_jg is the residual variance. The distribution option instead draws the normal density of the item at the factor mean of its own group, with mean nu_jg + lambda_jg * alpha_g and variance theta_jg. For an ordered-categorical indicator the vertical axis is the underlying response variable y*, nu is zero, the thresholds of the item are drawn as light reference lines, and the residual variance is not printed under the delta parameterization: it is recovered from the scale factor as

    theta_jg = 1 / delta_jg^2 - lambda_jg^2 * psi_g

    which follows from Var(y*) = 1 / delta_jg^2 and Var(y*) = lambda_jg^2 * psi_g + theta_jg. Under the theta parameterization the residual variance is printed and is used as it stands. Under the figure the page states, with the numbers, where the groups part: in slope, that is in the loading, in intercept or in threshold, and in residual standard deviation when the figure carries one. A difference smaller than .001 is not reported as a difference, and when nothing differs the page says that the lines coincide, since a figure of coincident lines shows only the one drawn last.

    The second figure puts one class of parameters of one model on a single axis: the items on the vertical axis, and on the horizontal one the estimate in each group with the interval

    estimate ± 1.959964 * standard error

    read from MODEL RESULTS, which is the 95 percent Wald interval Mplus itself reports under CINTERVAL. Whether a parameter is held equal across groups is read from TECH1, by the rule used for the ladder: one number in every group. Such a row carries one estimate, drawn once as an open diamond and labelled as held equal, because k coincident points would read as k groups that happen to agree when they are one parameter. A parameter fixed by the model, the loading that sets the metric of the factor for instance, is drawn once as an open marker without an interval and labelled as fixed. When TECH1 is missing the page falls back to the same estimate and the same standard error in every group and says that it did so. Two intervals that do not overlap are a hint about where to look; the test of the constraint is the difference test between the two models.

    Both figures are drawn in a coordinate system whose unit is the typographic point, and the viewBox spans width_mm / 25.4 * 72 units, so a type size given in points is that size in the exported figure at the stated width. The axes are black, with two spines and no box, ticks pointing outward and no gridlines; the only reference lines are the thresholds and, in the second figure, the zero, drawn lighter than any data element. The number of ticks is the largest for which the labels still leave a gap between them, and the number of decimals is the smallest at which every label is the value it stands for and no two labels are alike. Threshold labels sit in the margin beside the plotting area, where no curve reaches them, and are pushed apart when two thresholds fall close together. Each group has a color from the Okabe-Ito palette, a dash pattern and, if you ask for them, a marker shape, so that the groups stay apart in grayscale and under any color-vision deficiency; the legend has a band of its own under the plotting area and cannot overlap the data. Residual bands are drawn under the lines at a fill opacity of 0.16 for two groups and less for more, so the lines remain the most salient element. The figure is exported as SVG, which is vectorial and carries its width in millimeters, and as PNG at the stated width and a resolution of 300, 600 or 1200 dpi; both are produced from the figure on the screen, so what is saved is what is shown.

    The chi-square tail

    The p-value is the upper tail P(X ≥ x) of a chi-square distribution, computed from the regularized incomplete gamma function by its series expansion below the crossover and by its continued fraction above it. The upper tail is computed directly rather than as 1 − F(x), which loses every significant digit once p falls below about 1e-16.
