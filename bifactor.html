Bifactor indices

This page computes the indices normally reported for a bifactor confirmatory factor model — omega and omega hierarchical, explained common variance, percentage of uncontaminated correlations, construct replicability and factor determinacy — from the standardized loadings on one general factor and one or more specific factors. Loadings can be read from an Mplus output file, pasted as a matrix, or typed into a table.

What to request from Mplus. OUTPUT: STDYX; — every index on this page is defined on the standardized metric, so the completely standardized solution is what the parser reads. The residual variances printed in the same section are read as well; when they are absent, or present for only some items, they are recomputed as one minus the sum of the squared loadings for all of them, and the page says which of the two it used.

Reading the indices

The values below are guidance taken from the sources named, not decision rules. None of them replaces an evaluation of the model as a whole, and each was proposed for a particular family of measures and simulation conditions.

  • Rodriguez, Reise and Haviland (2016a) treat an explained common variance above about .70 together with a percentage of uncontaminated correlations above about .70 as pointing to a general factor strong enough that fitting a unidimensional model to the same data would distort the structural parameters only slightly.
  • Reise, Scheines, Widaman and Haviland (2013) discuss the joint role of the three quantities: when the percentage of uncontaminated correlations is below about .80, they read a general explained common variance above about .60 together with an omega hierarchical above about .70 as multidimensionality that is not severe enough to prevent interpreting the instrument as primarily unidimensional.
  • Bonifay, Reise, Scheines and Meijer (2015) relate the explained common variance to the size of the bias directly: in their conditions relative bias fell below 10% once the explained common variance exceeded about .70, and below 5% once it exceeded about .80.
  • Hancock and Mueller (2001) define construct replicability as the correlation between a factor and an optimally weighted composite of its indicators; values around .80 are discussed as an indication that the factor is well defined by the items measuring it.
  • Gorsuch (1983) discusses factor determinacy around .90 as the level below which factor score estimates should not be used in place of the factor itself.
  • Reise, Bonifay and Haviland (2013) point out that a low omega hierarchical subscale value makes a subscale score hard to interpret as a distinct construct, because most of its reliable variance belongs to the general factor rather than to the specific factor.

These indices describe the loading pattern of a model that has already been fitted. They cannot establish that a bifactor model is the correct account of the data, because they are computed from the bifactor solution itself and would be computed the same way from a solution that is wrong. Bonifay and Cai (2017) show that the bifactor item response model has a high fitting propensity: it fits a wide range of data patterns, including patterns produced by other structures and patterns that are random, so a good fit is weak evidence of a bifactor structure. Bonifay, Lane and Reise (2017) raise the same point for the bifactor model of psychopathology, together with the difficulty of interpreting specific factors whose reliable variance is small.

Methods and formulas

Write λik for the standardized loading of item i on factor k, with k = G for the general factor and k = 1 … S for the specific factors. An item is treated as loading on a factor when that loading is present and different from zero. The uniqueness of item i is

θ_i = 1 − Σ_k λ_ik²

with the sum taken over the general factor and over every specific factor the item loads on. When the standardized residual variances are read from the output, or typed into the table with the corresponding option checked, those values are used instead. A negative uniqueness is truncated at zero and reported as a warning, because a negative value is not a variance and would make the reproduced correlation matrix indefinite.

Omega and omega hierarchical

For a factor f, let If be the items loading on it. Omega is the proportion of the variance of the unit-weighted total of those items that is accounted for by all the common factors together:

ω_f = [ Σ_k ( Σ_{i∈I_f} λ_ik )² ] / [ Σ_{i∈I_f} θ_i + Σ_k ( Σ_{i∈I_f} λ_ik )² ]

Omega hierarchical keeps the same denominator and retains in the numerator only the factor of interest, so it is the proportion of the variance of that total attributable to one factor:

ω_H  = ( Σ_{i∈I_G} λ_iG )² / [ Σ_{i∈I_G} θ_i + Σ_k ( Σ_{i∈I_G} λ_ik )² ]
ω_HS = ( Σ_{i∈I_s} λ_is )² / [ Σ_{i∈I_s} θ_i + Σ_k ( Σ_{i∈I_s} λ_ik )² ]

For the general factor the items are all of them, so ω is the omega of the whole scale and ωH the part of it carried by the general factor; for a specific factor the items are those of the subscale, so ω is the omega of the subscale score and ωHS the part of it left once the general factor is partialled out. The general-factor omega is the omega of a multidimensional composite in the sense of Zinbarg, Revelle, Yovel and Li (2005). Relative omega is their ratio:

relative ω_f = ω_H(f) / ω_f

It is the share of the reliable variance of the composite that belongs to the factor of interest.

Explained common variance

The common variance of item i is Σk λik², and the explained common variance of a factor is its share of a sum of such quantities. Two sums are in use. Over all N items,

ECV_f = Σ_{i=1..N} λ_if² / Σ_{i=1..N} Σ_k λ_ik²

For the general factor this is the quantity usually called ECV. For a specific factor it is the form given by Stucky and Edelen (2015), often written ECV_SG, which measures the strength of that specific factor against all the common variance in the instrument, including the items that do not load on it. Restricting both sums to the items of the subscale gives the second form:

ECV_SS(s) = Σ_{i∈I_s} λ_is² / Σ_{i∈I_s} Σ_k λ_ik²

This is the strength of the specific factor within its own items. The share the general factor takes within the same items is reported alongside it:

ECV_GS(s) = Σ_{i∈I_s} λ_iG² / Σ_{i∈I_s} Σ_k λ_ik²

ECV_GS and ECV_SS add to one when the items of the subscale load on the general factor and on that specific factor only; when some of them also load on another specific factor the two shares add to less than one, and the remainder belongs to the other specific factors. The same ratio taken one item at a time is the item explained common variance of Stucky, Thissen and Edelen (2013):

I-ECV_i = λ_iG² / Σ_k λ_ik²

Percentage of uncontaminated correlations

A correlation between two items is contaminated, in the sense of Reise, Scheines, Widaman and Haviland (2013), when both items load on the same specific factor, since then the covariance between them carries specific variance as well as general variance. With a clean cluster structure, in which every item loads on the general factor and on at most one specific factor, the contaminated pairs can be counted subscale by subscale:

PUC = 1 − [ Σ_s n_s(n_s − 1)/2 ] / [ N(N − 1)/2 ]

where ns is the number of items in subscale s and N the number of items. Items that load on the general factor only enter N and belong to no subscale, which is what the formula requires. When an item loads on two or more specific factors the subscale-by-subscale count adds the same pair more than once and the formula returns too small a value, so a generalized version is computed as well, counting each pair of items that share at least one specific factor exactly once:

PUC* = 1 − |{ (i,j) : i < j and item i and item j share at least one specific factor }| / [ N(N − 1)/2 ]

The two agree exactly whenever no item loads on more than one specific factor. The page reports both and states which one applies to the pattern that was entered.

Construct replicability

H is the squared correlation between a factor and the optimally weighted composite of its indicators (Hancock & Mueller, 2001). With orthogonal factors and standardized loadings it reduces to

H_f = 1 / ( 1 + 1 / Σ_{i∈I_f} [ λ_if² / (1 − λ_if²) ] )

and it is computed here for the general factor over all items and for each specific factor over the items of its subscale. A standardized loading of exactly one makes the term undefined and is reported rather than computed.

Factor determinacy

Factor determinacy is the correlation between a factor and the factor score estimate obtained for it (Gorsuch, 1983). Collecting the loadings in the N × (S+1) matrix Λ and the uniquenesses in the diagonal matrix Θ, the model-reproduced correlation matrix under orthogonal factors is

Σ = Λ Λ′ + Θ

and the determinacies are the square roots of the diagonal of

Λ′ Σ⁻¹ Λ

This form assumes that the general factor and the specific factors are mutually orthogonal, which is the usual bifactor specification and the assumption under which every other index on this page is also computed. The square of the determinacy is reported as well, since it is the proportion of the variance of the factor score estimate that the factor accounts for.

Σ is factorized by Cholesky decomposition, which succeeds only if Σ is positive definite, and the linear systems Σ X = Λ are solved by forward and back substitution rather than by forming the inverse. When the factorization fails, the page reports that Σ is not positive definite, names the item at which the factorization broke down, and reports no determinacy at all instead of a number obtained from a fallback that would not be meaningful. A matrix that is only nearly singular is detected the same way through the size of the pivots relative to the largest diagonal entry.

Relative parameter bias

When the loadings of the same items from a unidimensional model are supplied, the relative parameter bias of item i is the difference between the loading in the unidimensional solution and the loading on the general factor in the bifactor solution, divided by the latter:

RPB_i = ( λ_i,unidimensional − λ_iG ) / λ_iG

A positive value therefore means that the unidimensional model gives the item a larger loading than the bifactor model does. This is the order used by Rodriguez, Reise and Haviland (2016b); the Bifactor Indices Calculator of Dueber (2017) takes the difference the other way round, so the values printed here have the opposite sign to the ones it prints, and the same absolute value. The average absolute relative parameter bias is the mean of |RPBi| over the items for which a unidimensional loading was supplied.

What the page does not do

Every formula above assumes orthogonal factors, continuous indicators on a standardized metric, and one general factor. Correlated specific factors, second-order and higher-order models, two-tier models with more than one general factor, and the extra terms that categorical indicators bring to the reliability of an observed total score are outside what is computed here. With categorical indicators the loadings read from the STDYX section refer to the latent response variables, so the indices describe the model on that metric and not the metric of the observed responses.

Three structures are refused rather than computed. An output whose indicators include a latent variable is a second-order or hierarchical model, and none of the formulas above applies to it; a second-order model can be rewritten in the equivalent bifactor form by the Schmid-Leiman transformation and its loadings pasted into the table. An output that prints the same item name twice under one BY heading would lose one of the two loadings when the table is built. An output with several groups holds a separate solution for each of them, and the page asks which group to read instead of taking the first one and reporting numbers for a group nobody chose. A specific factor with a single indicator is loaded and computed, because the arithmetic is defined, together with a message saying that its indices describe one item rather than a factor.
