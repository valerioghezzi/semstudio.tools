Interaction plots and simple slopes

This page reads an Mplus output file, draws the interaction between a predictor and a moderator, tests the simple slope of X on Y at chosen values of the moderator, and locates the Johnson–Neyman thresholds that fall inside the range of the moderator the data cover. Every number it computes with is read from that file, and none of them can be typed in.

What to request from Mplus. OUTPUT: SAMPSTAT STDYX TECH1 TECH3; for an interaction between observed variables, with TECH4 added when the interaction is between latent variables. TECH1 numbers the free parameters and TECH3 prints their covariance matrix: together they give the standard error of a simple slope and the Johnson–Neyman thresholds, and SAMPSTAT gives the means, standard deviations and observed minimum and maximum used for the levels of the moderator and for the axes.

What to do

  1. Open the Mplus input file of the model that contains the interaction, the file with the extension .inp, and add this line to it, exactly as it is written here: OUTPUT: SAMPSTAT STDYX TECH1 TECH3;
  2. Add TECH4 to that same line, so that it reads OUTPUT: SAMPSTAT STDYX TECH1 TECH3 TECH4;, only when the interaction is between two latent variables formed with XWITH. TECH4 prints the estimated means and variances of the latent variables, which are not in the sample statistics.
  3. Run that input in Mplus. The program writes a plain text file with the same name as the input and the extension .out, in the folder the input is in.
  4. Drag the .out file onto the box below, or press Choose the .out file and pick it there. The file is read inside your browser and nothing leaves your computer.
  5. Read the paragraph headed Output read. It names every regression found in MODEL RESULTS, the estimator, whether TECH1 and TECH3 are in the file, and any row the program printed as asterisks.
  6. Choose the dependent variable, the predictor whose slope is drawn, and how the moderator enters the model: one continuous variable, one categorical variable with numeric codes, or a set of dummy variables with one product term each.
  7. Choose the moderator and the term that carries the product of the predictor and the moderator. The page names the coefficients it read for them; those values cannot be changed here.
  8. Set the levels of a continuous moderator at which the slope is to be drawn. They start at one standard deviation below the mean, at the mean and at one standard deviation above it, all three read from the file, and you can change them because which levels to draw is a decision about the figure. The codes of a categorical moderator are read from the file and are not adjustable.
  9. Read the figure, the table of simple slopes under it, and the Johnson–Neyman section, which reports the values of the moderator at which the slope stops differing from zero.
  10. Set the labels, the axes, the printed width and the resolution, then download the figure as SVG or as PNG.

The page refuses a file that is empty, a file that carries none of the headings Mplus always writes, a file that ends before MODEL RESULTS because the run was interrupted, a run that did not converge, and an output produced with TYPE = TWOLEVEL, with latent classes, with more than one group or with ESTIMATOR = BAYES, whose MODEL RESULTS sections are laid out differently. It also refuses to report a test when TECH1 and TECH3 are absent, and to draw a categorical moderator whose codes are not in the file. Every number the page computes with is read from the output: there is no field on this page in which a coefficient, a standard error, a mean or a standard deviation can be typed.

Methods and formulas

The simple slope and its standard error

The model behind every figure on this page is the linear regression of Y on a predictor X, a moderator W and their product, written here with the notation of Aiken and West (1991):

Y = b0 + b1X + b2W + b3XW + e

Here Y is the dependent variable, b0 its intercept, X the predictor and b1 its coefficient, W the moderator and b2 its coefficient, b3 the coefficient of the product of X and W, and e the residual.

Collecting the terms in X gives the conditional regression of Y on X at a fixed value w of the moderator, whose slope, the simple slope, is a linear function of w:

Y = (b0 + b2w) + (b1 + b3w)X + e, s(w) = b1 + b3w

Here w is the value at which the moderator is held, b0 + b2w is the conditional intercept, and s(w) is the simple slope of X on Y at that value.

Because s(w) is linear in the estimates, its sampling variance follows from the covariance matrix of the estimates without any approximation beyond the one already made in treating that matrix as the asymptotic covariance matrix. This is the delta method applied to a linear function:

Var[s(w)] = Var(b1) + w2 Var(b3) + 2w Cov(b1, b3)

Here Var[s(w)] is the sampling variance of the simple slope at w, Var(b1) and Var(b3) are the sampling variances of the two coefficients, and Cov(b1, b3) is their sampling covariance, all three read from TECH3.

z = s(w)√Var[s(w)]

Here z is the test statistic of the simple slope at w, referred to the standard normal distribution.

The test uses the standard normal distribution, as Mplus does, so it is asymptotic rather than an exact t test, and in small samples it should be read with the caution that implies. When the moderator is categorical and represented by dummy variables, the slope in category j is b1 + gj, with gj the coefficient of the product of X with the dummy variable of that category, and its variance is read from the same matrix:

Var(b1 + gj) = Var(b1) + Var(gj) + 2 Cov(b1, gj)

Here gj is the coefficient of the product of X with the dummy variable of category j, Var(gj) its sampling variance, and Cov(b1, gj) its sampling covariance with b1.

The difference between the slopes of two non-reference categories i and j is gj − gi, with variance

Var(gj − gi) = Var(gi) + Var(gj) − 2 Cov(gi, gj)

Here gi and gj are the coefficients of the two product terms, and Cov(gi, gj) is their sampling covariance. The difference between category j and the reference category is gj itself.

The Johnson–Neyman quadratic

Johnson and Neyman (1936) asked for which values of the moderator the conditional effect is distinguishable from zero. Setting the square of the test statistic equal to the square of the critical value z.975 = 1.96 and clearing the denominator turns that question into a quadratic equation in w (Bauer & Curran, 2005; Preacher, Curran & Bauer, 2006):

[s(w)]2 = z2 Var[s(w)], that is aw2 + bw + c = 0

Here s(w) is the simple slope at w, z is the critical value 1.96, and a, b and c are the three coefficients of the quadratic given next.

a = b32 − z2 Var(b3), b = 2[b1b3 − z2 Cov(b1, b3)], c = b12 − z2 Var(b1)

Here b1 and b3 are the coefficients of X and of the product, Var(b1), Var(b3) and Cov(b1, b3) their sampling variances and covariance, and z the critical value 1.96.

The quantity q(w) = aw2 + bw + c is positive exactly where the simple slope differs from zero at the 5% level. When a is positive the region of significance lies outside the two roots, when a is negative it lies between them; this page also evaluates q at the midpoint of each interval and reports a warning if the two ways of deciding disagree. Only roots that fall inside the range of the moderator the data actually cover are reported and drawn, because a threshold outside that range describes no observable case. That range is read from the univariate sample statistics when the output contains them, and is otherwise taken as the mean plus or minus three standard deviations, both read from the file; the page states which of the two was used. The procedure does not apply to a categorical moderator, whose intermediate values do not exist.

The standardized solution for a product term

In a standardized solution the coefficient of a product term is not obtained by dividing by the standard deviation of the product. The STDYX section of Mplus divides every coefficient by the standard deviation of its own predictor, and for the product that predictor is XW, whose standard deviation is not SD(X) SD(W). Wen, Marsh and Hau (2010) show that the appropriate standardized solution rescales the product coefficient by the standard deviations of the two components instead:

b1′ = b1 σXσY, b2′ = b2 σWσY, b3′ = b3 σX σWσY

Here b1′, b2′ and b3′ are the standardized coefficients of X, of W and of the product, b1, b2 and b3 the unstandardized ones, and σX, σW and σY the standard deviations of X, of W and of Y.

Under this rescaling the standardized simple slope of X on Y at a value w of the moderator is b1′ + b3′w / σW, so the standardized coefficients and the standardized simple slopes describe the same equation, and all three are unchanged when X, W or Y is measured in different units. The value of b1′ is the standardized effect of X at W = 0, which is the mean of W only when the moderator is centered; with latent variables, whose means Mplus fixes at zero, it always is. The page reports both columns and states which is which: for X and for W the two agree, and for the product they differ by construction.

When the interaction is between latent variables defined with XWITH, Mplus does not always print a standardized solution, because the product is not a variable of the model and has no estimated variance of its own. The total variance of Y is then reconstructed from the model under joint normality of X and W, using the variance of a product of two normal variables given by Bohrnstedt and Goldberger (1969):

Var(XW) = σ2Xσ2W + σ2XW + μ2Xσ2W + μ2Wσ2X + 2μXμWσXW

Here Var(XW) is the variance of the product, σ2X and σ2W are the variances of X and W, σXW is their covariance, and μX and μW are their means.

Cov(X, XW) = μWσ2X + μXσXW, Cov(W, XW) = μXσ2W + μWσXW

The symbols are those of the previous formula: the covariance of each component with the product is built from the mean of the other component, the variance of the component itself and the covariance between the two.

σ2Y = b12σ2X + b22σ2W + b32Var(XW) + 2b1b2σXW + 2b1b3Cov(X, XW) + 2b2b3Cov(W, XW) + σ2e

Here σ2Y is the total variance of Y implied by the model, b1, b2 and b3 are the three coefficients, the variances and covariances are those defined above, and σ2e is the residual variance of Y.

This is the same distributional assumption on which the latent moderated structural equations approach of Klein and Moosbrugger (2000) rests, and the reconstruction is reported as a reconstruction whenever it is used.

Predicted values and confidence bands

Each line in the figure is the predicted value of Y as a function of X at one value of the moderator. When the model contains predictors other than X, W and their product, they are held at their sample means as read from the file, and the product of each coefficient with its mean is added to the intercept; when a mean is not in the output the page says so and holds that predictor at zero. The confidence band around a line is the 95% interval for the conditional mean, computed from the same parameter covariance matrix as a quadratic form:

Var(Ŷ | x, w) = r′Vr

Here Ŷ is the predicted mean at the values x of the predictor and w of the moderator, r is the row of the design matrix at that point, which holds a one for the intercept, x, w, their product and the mean of every other predictor, and V is the covariance matrix of the estimates from TECH3.

Ŷ ± 1.96 √Var(Ŷ | x, w)

Here the two limits are the ends of the 95% interval for the conditional mean at that point, and 1.96 is the .975 quantile of the standard normal distribution.

The band requires the intercept, X, W and the product to be located in TECH1, and every other predictor of Y as well; when one of them cannot be located the band is omitted and the reason is stated under the figure.

What TECH1 and TECH3 contain

TECH1 prints the parameter specification: each free parameter of the model receives a number, and the numbers are laid out in the matrices of the general model, so that the coefficient of X in the regression of Y appears as an entry of Β or Γ in row Y and column X, and the intercept of Y appears in α or ν. Mplus prints these matrices in blocks of five columns, repeating the name of the matrix above each block, so a parser that stops at the first block loses every column from the sixth onwards; this page reads every block and keeps the column headings of each. TECH3 prints the covariance matrix of the parameter estimates, indexed by the same numbers. Reading the two together gives the exact sampling variances and covariance that the delta method needs. When TECH3 is absent, the page falls back on three simple slopes computed in MODEL CONSTRAINT as NEW parameters, from which Var(b1), Var(b3) and Cov(b1, b3) can be recovered by solving a linear system in three unknowns; those values carry the rounding of the three printed decimals, so TECH3 is preferred whenever it is available. A NEW parameter is used only when its name identifies it as a simple slope, so that an indirect or total effect that happens to fall near the same value is never mistaken for one.

Reading the output file

Files are read as raw bytes and decoded by inspecting the byte-order mark and by testing whether the content is valid UTF-8, falling back on Windows-1252, which is what Mplus writes on Windows. Fortran exponents of the form 0.123D-03 are converted before conversion to a number, a row of asterisks in place of a value is reported as unread rather than silently dropped, and the sentinel 999.000 on all four columns is treated as a value Mplus could not compute. The page refuses two-level, mixture, multiple-group and Bayesian outputs, whose MODEL RESULTS sections are organized differently; a file that ends before MODEL RESULTS, which comes from a run that was interrupted; a file with none of the headings Mplus always writes; an empty file; and a run that did not reach convergence. Each refusal names what was found. The estimator is read from SUMMARY OF ANALYSIS, and from the echoed input only when that section does not state it, in which case the page says so, because the estimator asked for in the input is not always the one the run used. It is never assumed.

Every quantity the page computes with is read from that file: the coefficients and their intercept from MODEL RESULTS, the means, variances and observed ranges from the univariate and the sample statistics or from TECH4, the codes of a categorical moderator from the same statistics, and the sampling variances and covariances from TECH1 with TECH3. None of them can be typed in, and none of them can be edited after being read. What remains adjustable is the description of the figure: which levels of a continuous moderator to draw, the labels, the axes, the type size, the printed width and the resolution of the export.

Self-test

Opening this page with ?selftest=1 appended to its address runs its numerical checks in the browser and prints the outcome and the number of checks at the top, with every check listed under it. They cover the two-sided normal tail against published values from z = 1 to z = 8; the simple slope and its delta-method variance over 2400 random problems, against the quadratic form written out separately and against a Monte Carlo of 200000 draws from the sampling distribution of the estimates; the identity on which the Johnson–Neyman quadratic rests, checked on 401 grid points in each of 2400 problems, together with the requirement that the test statistic change side of the critical value at the reported roots and nowhere else; a quadratic with no real root, with a discriminant of exactly zero, with a vanishing quadratic term and with two real roots; a slope significant over the whole range and one significant nowhere; the standardized transformation of a product term; the variance of a predicted mean, against a double loop and against a Monte Carlo; the simple slopes of a categorical moderator with three and with five categories and every difference between them; the refusal of two-level, mixture, multiple-group and Bayesian outputs; the parsers for TECH1 across twelve columns, for TECH3 with Fortran D exponents, for the univariate sample statistics and for the univariate proportions and counts of a categorical variable; the codes of a moderator dummy coded 0/1 and 1/2 and with three, five and ten categories, and the refusal to draw one whose codes are not in the file; and a moderator with no variance.
