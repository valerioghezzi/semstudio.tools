Invariance Syntax Builder

This page writes the Mplus input for a sequence of identified multiple-group confirmatory factor models, for continuous indicators and for ordered-categorical indicators. For continuous indicators it writes the configural, metric, scalar and strict models and the optional structural steps; for ordered-categorical indicators it writes the sequence of Wu and Estabrook (2016) as applied by Svetina, Rutkowski and Rutkowski (2020), in which each level is identified on its own rather than by adding constraints to an identified baseline.

What to request from Mplus. Every input written here ends with OUTPUT: TECH1 TECH4 STDYX;. TECH1 prints the parameter specification group by group, which is the direct way to check that each equality constraint landed on the parameter it was meant for; TECH4 prints the estimated factor means, variances and covariances, which are the quantities the later levels license you to compare; STDYX gives the standardized loadings.

Indicators
One factor per line, written as it would be written in Mplus: F1 BY x1 x2 x3 x4;. The hyphen range notation is read, so F1 BY x1-x4; is the same definition. Semicolons may be left out. Names may be up to eight characters, must begin with a letter and may contain letters, digits and underscores. Each item may belong to one factor only.

The inputs

! What this page cannot do. The syntax written below is a starting point to be read and checked against your data and your model before it is run. The page cannot verify that a model is identified for every structure that can be typed into it: cross-loadings, correlated residuals, an item belonging to more than one factor, factors with one or two indicators and empirically singular groups all change the identification argument, and the conditions used here are those given for a clustered pattern of non-negative loadings with each item on one factor.

Why the ordered-categorical sequence is not the familiar one

With continuous indicators the observed variable has a metric of its own. Its mean and its variance are estimated in every group, so an intercept and a residual variance can be estimated in every group as well, and the sequence configural, metric, scalar, strict adds one type of constraint at a time to a model that is already identified.

With ordered categories nothing is observed but the proportion of respondents in each category and the association between items. The model puts a normally distributed latent response variable behind each item and cuts it at thresholds, and that latent response has no metric of its own: its location and its scale are conventions. Wu and Estabrook (2016) write the transformation that changes those conventions without changing the probability of any response pattern. It has four parts in each group: a shift and a rescaling for every latent response, and a shift and a rescaling for every factor, so 2(p + m) constraints per group are needed to identify a model with p items and m factors.

The consequence is that the identification condition of a baseline model is not neutral with respect to the invariance tests that follow it. Once loadings are held equal across groups, a condition such as fixing the variance of every latent response to one in every group stops being a free choice of metric and becomes a restriction, and the same invariance hypothesis tested from two differently identified baselines gives two different chi-squares. Wu and Estabrook show a two-group example in which loading invariance is rejected under two identification conditions and retained under a third, with the same data and the same true parameters. Their answer is to identify each level of invariance separately, which is what this page writes.

Three consequences follow for the sequence itself.

  • Invariance of loadings alone, of intercepts alone, and of residual variances alone cannot be tested. A model with only one of these constrained is statistically equivalent to the configural model, so the difference test has no degrees of freedom to spend. Thresholds have to be dealt with first.
  • How much of threshold invariance can be tested depends on the number of categories. A shift and a rescaling of one latent response can always be chosen to make one threshold match, and can be chosen to make two thresholds match, but not three. An item with C categories therefore contributes (C − 3)(G − 1) degrees of freedom to the test of threshold invariance across G groups: none at all for a binary or three-category item, one per extra group for a four-category item.
  • Binary items are a separate case. With one threshold per item, threshold invariance and threshold-and-loading invariance are both statistically equivalent to the configural model (Wu & Estabrook, 2016, Propositions 3 and 6), and the model that holds thresholds invariant while freeing the intercepts and the scales of the other groups is not even identified. When any item is binary this page drops those two levels and goes from the configural model to the model with invariant thresholds, loadings and intercepts.

The two articles differ in how far they take the sequence and in what they call the levels. Wu and Estabrook (2016) name each model by the parameters it holds invariant and set out the whole hierarchy of sixteen models, of which ten are statistically distinct for polytomous items and five for binary items. Svetina, Rutkowski and Rutkowski (2020) follow the same identification conditions but write out three models only, which they name after the propositions they come from: the baseline model, Proposition 4 for threshold invariance and Proposition 7 for threshold and loading invariance. They also point out that the resulting order — thresholds first, then loadings — differs from the order in which applied work usually proceeds, where loadings are tested before thresholds and intercepts. One detail in their tutorial is a slip: the text and the annotation of the Proposition 7 input say that the item intercept is fixed at one in the first group and give [y1-y4@1];, while Condition 19 of Wu and Estabrook, which that step implements, sets those intercepts to zero. This page writes @0.
