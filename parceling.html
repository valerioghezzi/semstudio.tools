Item Parceling

This page builds item parcels for a latent construct by the strategies set out by Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022), inspects how far the parcels are balanced in strength and in size, and writes the Mplus DEFINE syntax that creates them together with a measurement model skeleton. The item names, their loadings, the facet each item belongs to and the pairs of items that share variance beyond the factor are all read from the output of the item-level model; nothing about the items is typed on this page.

What to request from Mplus. OUTPUT: STDYX MODINDICES; on an item-level model fitted first. The standardized loadings are what the balancing strategy orders the items by, and the modification indices are what the purposeful strategy uses to decide which items belong in the same parcel. Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) treat the item-level model as the step that comes before any parcel is built. A warning printed by Mplus is reported and does not remove a result the file supports; what removes a result is a quantity that is missing or inadmissible.

What to do

  1. Fit the model at the item level in Mplus, with every item as an indicator of the construct it belongs to, and add this line to the input file, exactly as it is written here: OUTPUT: STDYX MODINDICES;
  2. Run that input in Mplus. The program writes a plain text file with the same name as the input and the extension .out, in the same folder as the input.
  3. Drag the .out file onto the box below, or click that box, or move the focus to it and press Enter, to choose the file from your computer. It is read inside your browser and nothing leaves your computer.
  4. Choose which items are to be parceled: those of one factor, or those of every factor at once, each factor then taken as a facet of one construct. The item names, their standardized loadings and their facet all come from that choice.
  5. Choose how the items are to be allocated, how many parcels are wanted, and whether a parcel is the mean or the sum of its items. These are decisions about the scheme, not values from the file.
  6. Press Build the parcels from this file, then read the allocation table, the Mplus input written under it, and the paragraph that describes what was done.

The page refuses a file that has no STDYX Standardization section, because the loadings it orders the items by are the standardized ones. It refuses the purposeful strategy when the output carries no modification indices, because the pairs of items that belong together are read from them. It refuses the two facet strategies unless every item carries a facet, which means choosing every factor at once in a file that holds more than one factor. In each case it says which OUTPUT option supplies what is missing.

Load the Mplus output

Drop the Mplus .out file of the item-level model here, or press Enter to choose one.

Windows-1252, UTF-8 and UTF-16 output files are all decoded. Only the first group of a multiple-group output is read.

One factor gives its items and their loadings on it. Every factor at once gives the items of all of them, each item with its loading on the factor it belongs to and that factor as its facet, which is what the two facet strategies need.

No file has been read yet, so there are no items to parcel.

Choose the scheme

With more than one draw the page reports how much the balance of the parcels varies over draws and shows the draw at the median, naming its seed.

The pairs come from the modification indices in the output: a residual covariance between two items, or cross-loadings of opposite sign on another factor. Items linked directly or through a third item go into the same parcel. This number selects which of the printed indices to act on; it changes nothing that was estimated.

A parcel is a variable this page creates, so its name is not in the output; the construct keeps the name the output gives the factor.

Allocation

The parcels, the items in each of them and their balance appear here once an output file has been read and the parcels have been built.

Wording for a manuscript

The paragraph below states what was done, in the terms the strategy is described in, and is meant to be edited rather than copied unchanged: the name of the scale and the sample are yours to supply.

When parceling is appropriate and when it distorts the model

Everything in this section is an account of what the cited authors argue. None of it is a rule, and none of it settles a particular application.

The question the decision turns on

Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) make the decision turn on the aim of the analysis. If the aim is to study the behavior of the items — developing an instrument, examining how a particular item works — parceling should not be done, because the items are the object of study. If the aim is to model the relationships among latent variables, they treat parceling as a viable technique for recovering population values, and refer that conclusion to Little, Cunningham, Shahar and Widaman (2002). The same authors state that a parceling scheme has to be justified, executed well, and reported transparently, and that a parceling scheme chosen without a rationale is not recommended.

The items in a parcel have to be unidimensional

The strategies for a unidimensional item set — random assignment, purposeful parceling, the balancing technique — are described by Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) for items that measure one construct. Random assignment in particular is said to rest on the strong assumption that the items are interchangeable in the population, and to be recommended for unidimensional indicators, or for a large set of items with high communality, when sampling variability is low; the sample size they attach to that condition is above about 200. When items are not interchangeable, the allocation is no longer arbitrary and the choice of allocation begins to matter.

The arguments against parceling are reviewed at length in Little, Cunningham, Shahar and Widaman (2002) and Matsunaga (2008), to which Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) refer. The consequence of parceling items that are not unidimensional is the main argument against parceling that these authors report, and they attribute it to Bandalos (2002): with multidimensional or multifaceted constructs, parcels can bias the estimated factor loadings in the measurement model and confuse the interpretation of the structural relations. Their own position is that this holds when parcels are created randomly or improperly, and that parcels built by a strategy suited to the dimensionality of the items — the two multidimensional strategies below — can make the structure of a multidimensional construct easier rather than harder to interpret, a position they attribute to Graham, Tatterson and Widaman (2000).

What to do with a multidimensional item set

Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) describe two schemes, and the choice between them is a choice about what the latent construct is to mean. Both have older names, which they list: the first has been called homogeneous parceling, the isolated uniqueness strategy (Hall, Snell & Foust, 1999) and factored homogeneous item dimensions (Comrey, 1961); the second heterogeneous parceling and the distributed uniqueness strategy (Hall, Snell & Foust, 1999), and its present name comes from Kishton and Widaman (1994). In facet-representative parceling, the items of one facet go into one parcel, so the uniqueness of the facet is isolated inside that parcel and the common factor captures only what the facets share; this is an implicit way of estimating what would otherwise be a higher-order model, and they recommend it when the interest is in the higher-order construct alone. In domain-representative parceling, the items of each facet are spread over the parcels, so the secondary variance becomes common to the parcels and enters the latent factor along with the original common variance; they recommend it when each facet's specific variance is expected to relate to other constructs in the model beyond the variance the facets share, referring that recommendation to Kishton and Widaman (1994) and Little, Rhemtulla, Gibson and Schoemann (2013). They also report that in the comparison of the two schemes on an Internal–External Locus of Control measure by Kishton and Widaman (1994) the domain-representative scheme performed better.

The effect on fit and on parameter estimates

Two effects are separated in the source. The first is that fit improves. Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) report the demonstration in their own Section 3, where an item-level model with poor fit becomes a parcel-level model with good fit, and they attribute a general version of the finding to Landis, Beal and Tesluk (2000): improvements in fit are generally the result of a stronger measurement model. They also note that a model with four parceled indicators per construct rather than three is overidentified and that the extra degrees of freedom typically produce an arbitrary improvement in fit.

The second effect is the argument that parcels camouflage misspecification, which they attribute to Bandalos (2002) and to Marsh, Lüdtke, Nagengast, Morin and von Davier (2013): cross-loadings and residual correlations are harder to detect with parcels, so estimates may be biased, and the better fit may be read as the absence of misspecification. Against this they cite the simulation of Rhemtulla (2016), in which parcel-level models produced similar or less biased estimates more often than item-level models, and in which parcel-level models had lower power to detect a within-factor misspecification or a single cross-loading but higher power to detect multiple cross-loadings and misspecifications in the structural model.

A third effect concerns the allocation itself. Results can vary with the parceling scheme, which Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) list as an argument against parceling and answer in two parts: the parceling decisions should be described so that they can be reproduced, and the variability of the results across allocations of items to parcels can itself be examined and reported, for which they refer to Sterba (2019) and Sterba and MacCallum (2010). Sterba and Rights (2017) is cited by them for the point that a random scheme requires the combinations to be generated and evaluated and a decision rule to be stated for what counts as an optimal set of parcels.

What they recommend reporting

The review of the developmental literature in Section 5 of Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) found that reporting was often incomplete, and states what a report should contain: the results of the preliminary analyses used to inform the strategy, such as an item-level exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis; a rationale for the technique and for the number of parcels; and the items combined in each parcel. Where space does not allow it, they suggest supplementary materials. On checking the parcels against the items, they recommend modeling the data at the item level first, to identify the causes of misspecification and to inform how the parcels are built, attributing that recommendation to Bandalos and Finney (2001), Little, Rhemtulla, Gibson and Schoemann (2013) and Rhemtulla (2016); and they recommend examining local misfit in the parcel-level model, noting that the RMSEA and the SRMR were found by Rhemtulla (2016) to increase in parcel-level models relative to item-level models when there was misspecification.

Two further pieces of guidance from the same source bear on what this page writes. They recommend averaging the items rather than summing them, so that the parcel keeps the metric of the response scale; with effects-coded scaling the mean of the construct is then the mean of the items. And they recommend against proration, that is against averaging the items that are present when some are missing, on the ground that proration is a within-person mean imputation whose assumptions are often untenable and which is associated with bias even under data missing completely at random, referring to Lee, Bartholow, McCarthy, Pedersen and Sher (2015), Mazza, Enders and Ruehlman (2015) and Schafer and Graham (2002). Their suggestion is that the parcel be computed only when all its items are present, and that the incomplete items be entered as auxiliary variables when full information maximum likelihood is used.

How many parcels, and how many items in each

These are the recommendations of Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022), reported as such.

  • Three parcels per construct is the configuration they call optimum. With three indicators the construct is just identified once a scaling constraint is chosen, so there are no within-construct degrees of freedom and all the misfit of the model is between constructs.
  • Four or more parcels leave the construct overidentified. They note that the leftover degrees of freedom typically give an arbitrary improvement in fit, and that overidentified constructs have been known to be awkward under model constraints because the orientation among the loadings can shift.
  • Two parcels are acceptable in longitudinal and multiple-group models, because the constraints that establish measurement invariance identify the loadings and intercepts. In a single-group, single-occasion model a two-indicator construct is underidentified; the parameters can be identified by constraining the two loadings equal, and they report simulation work by Little, Lindenberger and Nesselroade (1999) in which such constraints were on average unbiased.
  • The number of items in a parcel does not have to be the same for every parcel, and not every item has to be assigned to a parcel. An item that sits at the center of the construct — their example is “I feel lonely” in a loneliness scale — can be left as an indicator on its own.
  • They give no minimum number of items per parcel. Their examples run from two items upward, and their remark on designing a measure with parceling in mind is that multiples of three are a good starting point for the number of items, with at least three indicators for each subscale.

Methods and procedures

Each strategy below is the procedure described in the section of Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) named with it, together with the rules this page uses to settle the cases the description leaves open. Those rules are marked as such.

Reading the output file

Everything about the items is read from the .out file of the item-level model, and nothing about them can be entered on this page. The STDYX Standardization section supplies the item names and their standardized loadings, SUMMARY OF ANALYSIS supplies the name of the data file that the written input then names in turn, the input echoed under INPUT INSTRUCTIONS supplies the NAMES and MISSING statements that the written input repeats, and MODEL MODIFICATION INDICES supplies the pairs of items the purposeful strategy keeps together. Windows-1252, UTF-8 and UTF-16 encodings are decoded, Fortran D exponents are accepted, an estimate printed with asterisks in place of its standard error is still read, and the 999.000 sentinel, which Mplus prints for a quantity it could not compute, is discarded rather than read as a loading of 999. Only the first group of a multiple-group output is read, and when the invariance shortcut has written several models into one file the first of them is read.

Choosing one factor gives its items and their loadings on it. Choosing every factor at once gives the items of all of them and writes the name of the factor an item loads on as its facet, which is the input the two multidimensional strategies need; an item that loads on more than one factor is assigned the factor on which its loading is largest in absolute value, and is named in the message, because such an item is not cleanly a member of one facet. The construct in the written input is the factor the items came from; with every factor taken as a facet the output holds no name for the construct the facets share, so the input calls it G.

The balancing technique

Section 3.2.6. The items are put in decreasing order of their loading. The first goes to parcel 1, the second to parcel 2, the third to parcel 3; the fourth then goes back to parcel 3, the fifth to parcel 2, the sixth to parcel 1, and the sequence turns again. With six items and three parcels this is exactly the pairing the source describes, the highest-loading item with the lowest, the second highest with the second lowest, and so on. Rules used here for what the description leaves open: ties are broken by the order in which the items appear in the output, so the same input always gives the same allocation; an item with no loading is placed after every item that has one, and the page says how many items that applies to; and a negative loading counts as the lowest, which is what the ordering says, with a separate warning that the item should be reverse-coded first.

Random assignment

Section 3.2.1. The items are shuffled by the Fisher–Yates algorithm and then cut into parcels whose sizes are as equal as they can be. The numbers come from a counter-based generator rather than from Math.random, so an allocation is reproduced exactly from its seed; a seed of zero is a valid state. With more than one draw the page repeats the allocation over consecutive seeds, records for each the range of the parcel loading sums, and shows the draw at the median of that distribution, naming the seed that produced it. The median draw is shown rather than the best one because selecting the allocation that looks best is the data-driven step the source asks to be justified. The dispersion reported is the dispersion of the balance of the loadings, which is what can be computed without fitting anything; the parcel-allocation variability of Sterba (2019) and Sterba and MacCallum (2010) is variability in the parameter estimates and the fit, and obtaining it means fitting the model under each allocation.

Purposeful parceling

Sections 3.2.4 and 3.2.5, followed by the balancing technique for the items that were not kept together, which is the combination described at the end of Section 3.2.6. Section 3.2.4 names three patterns in the modification indices of the item-level model: a pair of items with a noticeably high cross-loading on the same other construct, which can be parceled together so that the cross-loading is estimated at the parcel level; a pair of items whose cross-loadings on another construct are of similar size and opposite sign, which cancel when the two are averaged; and a pair of items with a high residual covariance. This page acts on the second and the third, both read from the output rather than named by the reader: the opposite-sign pair is printed under BY Statements and the residual covariance under WITH Statements. The first pattern is not acted on, because whether a shared cross-loading is to be kept and estimated or removed is a decision about the model that the indices alone do not settle; two items that both load on another construct are left to be placed by their loadings, and the cross-loading is visible in the printed indices. The source also says that cross-loadings usually take priority over correlated residuals; this page gives no priority to either pattern, since every pair that reaches the threshold is kept together. Rules used here: a pair counts when its modification index reaches the threshold set above the table, which selects among the indices Mplus printed and changes nothing that was estimated; for the cross-loading pattern the two items are paired on the same other factor, largest positive expected parameter change with largest negative, and only when the smaller of the two changes is at least half the larger in absolute value, since a pair of very unequal cross-loadings does not cancel; items linked directly, or linked through a third item, form one group, so that no item can be claimed by two parcels. The largest group is then seeded into the first parcel, the next into the second, and so on, with a group beyond the last parcel going to the parcel then holding the fewest items; each remaining item, taken in decreasing order of loading, goes to the parcel with the fewest items, ties broken by the smaller sum of loadings. Because each group occupies a parcel of its own, at most the number of groups plus the number of items in no group can be filled, and asking for more parcels than that is refused with the number that can be filled.

The operation this strategy performs is also the objection raised against it. Placing two items with a high residual covariance in the same parcel removes that covariance from the parcel-level model, and Coffman and MacCallum (2005) and Little, Cunningham, Shahar and Widaman (2002) hold that aggregating items whose residuals are correlated hides the misspecification and inflates the fit of the parcel-level model. Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) present the same operation as the way to keep local dependence from distorting the measurement model, on the condition that the items are unidimensional and that the decision is reported. Both readings describe the same consequence: the parcel-level model is no longer able to show what was folded into a parcel. The page therefore prints, beside the allocation, every pair it kept together with the modification index it was read from, so that the pairs can be reported and the fit of the parcel-level model interpreted with them in view.

Facet-representative parceling

Section 3.3.1. Every parcel is built from the items of one facet. When the number of parcels equals the number of facets there is one parcel per facet. When more parcels are asked for, the extra ones are given to the facets with the most items per parcel, and the items of a facet split over two or more parcels are divided by the balancing rule; this follows the source's remark that a facet with more than three scores can itself be parceled. Fewer parcels than facets is refused, because two facets sharing a parcel would defeat the purpose of the scheme, which is to isolate the uniqueness of each facet inside its own parcel. A facet with a single item yields a parcel that is that item, which the source allows and the page reports.

Domain-representative parceling

Section 3.3.2. The items of each facet are spread over the parcels. Rule used here: within a facet the items are taken in decreasing order of loading, and each goes to a parcel that does not yet hold an item of that facet in the current round; among those the parcel with the fewest items is chosen, ties broken by the smaller sum of loadings. A new round begins when every parcel has received one. This guarantees that whenever a facet has at least as many items as there are parcels, every parcel receives at least one item from it. When a facet has fewer items than there are parcels the guarantee cannot hold, and the page names the parcels that lack a facet and the facets they lack, because the parcels are then not interchangeable: they do not sample the same facets.

What became of the a priori scheme

Section 3.3.3 describes an allocation fixed in advance by the design of the measure. That allocation is a decision of the researcher and is nowhere in an Mplus output, so this page does not offer it: a scheme typed in here would be the one input that could not be traced to the file. An a priori design is written directly as DEFINE statements in the input, and the source's own warning applies to it, that such a scheme should be checked against the covariance pattern actually observed before it is used.

Balance, and the comparison shown beside it

For each parcel the page reports the number of items in it, the mean of their loadings and the sum of their loadings. Writing Pp for the set of items allocated to parcel p and np for how many there are, the two quantities are

λ¯p=1np∑iλi

Here λ̄p is the mean loading of parcel p, np the number of items in it, the sum over i runs over those items, and λi is the standardized loading of item i read from the output.

Sp=∑iλi

Here Sp is the sum of the loadings of parcel p, the sum over i running over the same items.

The mean says how strongly the parcel marks the construct; the sum is what an equal split of total strength would equalize, and the balancing technique reduces the spread of it, that is

range⁡(S)=maxpSp−minpSp

Here range(S) is the spread reported under the allocation table, and the maximum and the minimum are taken over the parcels of the scheme.

A plain sequential split — the items cut into consecutive blocks in the order they were read from the file — is summarized beside the allocation for comparison. Over 2,500 generated problems, each with 4 to 200 items and 2 to 20 parcels drawn uniformly and standardized loadings drawn uniformly between .30 and .90, the balancing technique gave a smaller spread of parcel loading sums than that split in 98.2% of them and the same spread in the remaining 1.8%; the median reduction was 80.4% of the spread the sequential split produced. The page warns when the mean loadings of the parcels range over more than .10; that threshold is a display convention of this page and not a criterion from the literature. It also reports when the parcels differ in size by more than one item, which is not a fault — the source states that parcels need not hold the same number of items — but does mean that a larger parcel is more reliable than a smaller one and will carry a larger loading for that reason alone.

The syntax, and what is checked in it

A parcel written as an average appears as the sum of its items in parentheses divided by their number, rather than through a function that aggregates them, so that the value of the parcel depends on every item in it. The reason is the source's recommendation against proration; the input carries a comment asking that the covariance coverage the program prints be compared with the coverage of the least complete item of each parcel, which is how proration would show itself. Names longer than eight characters, names that are Mplus operators or keywords, names that repeat, and names that are not legal variable names are refused before anything is written, with the offending names given. A parcel name that would collide with an item name is changed and the change is visible in the table. The NAMES and MISSING statements are those of the item-level model, read from the input echoed at the head of the output, because the written input reads the same data file and has to declare the same columns; when the output does not echo them, or the NAMES statement uses a range that cannot be expanded without the data file, the items alone are listed and the input says that the line has to be completed. The generated input is then read back and checked against what it was meant to say: that NAMES lists those columns and holds every item, that USEVARIABLES lists the parcels and no item, that no line of the MODEL command carries more than one parameter label, that there is one DEFINE statement per parcel using exactly the items allocated to it and, for an average, dividing by the right number, that every variable used in DEFINE is declared or created earlier, that the BY statement names every parcel, that no line exceeds the ninety characters the program reads, and that no name exceeds eight characters. The checks are written against the requirement rather than against the generator, so that a generator wrong twice in the same way does not pass.

What the page does not do

It does not fit any model. It cannot tell whether the items in a parcel are unidimensional, cannot compute modification indices, and cannot compare a parcel-level solution with an item-level one; all three have to be done in Mplus, and the source treats the item-level model as the step that comes first. It does not read raw data, so it cannot compute the parcels themselves, only the syntax that computes them, and it cannot check for missing values or for items that are keyed reverse-keyed. It does not accept an allocation, a loading or an item name typed by hand, so an item that is not in the output cannot be parceled here. The loadings it orders items by are the standardized loadings of the model that was fitted, so they are only as good as that model. Only one construct is handled at a time; a model with several constructs means running the page once for each and merging the inputs. The two multidimensional strategies take the facet structure as given rather than testing it. Nothing here decides whether a construct should be parceled at all.
