Item parceling

This page builds item parcels for a latent construct by the strategies set out by Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022), inspects how far the parcels are balanced in strength and in size, and writes the Mplus DEFINE syntax that creates them together with a measurement model skeleton. Item names, loadings and facet labels can be typed, pasted, or read from the STDYX section of an Mplus output file.

What to request from Mplus. OUTPUT: STDYX MODINDICES; on an item-level model fitted first. The standardized loadings are what the balancing strategy orders the items by, and the modification indices are what the purposeful strategy uses to decide which items belong in the same parcel. Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) treat the item-level model as the step that comes before any parcel is built.

Items

One item per line. The first field is the item name; the fields after it, separated by tabs, commas, semicolons or spaces, are optional: a standardized loading or item-total correlation, a facet label, and an a priori parcel label. A header row naming the columns (item, loading, facet, parcel) is used when it is present; without one the fields are read in that order.

When parceling is appropriate and when it distorts the model

Everything in this section is an account of what the cited authors argue. None of it is a rule, and none of it settles a particular application.

The question the decision turns on

Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) put the decision on the aim of the analysis. If the aim is to study the behavior of the items — developing an instrument, examining how a particular item works — parceling should not be done, because the items are the object of study. If the aim is to model the relationships among latent variables, they treat parceling as a viable technique for recovering population values, and refer that conclusion to Little, Cunningham, Shahar and Widaman (2002). The same authors state that a parceling scheme has to be justified, executed well, and reported transparently, and that misguided parceling is never recommended.

The items in a parcel have to be unidimensional

The strategies for a unidimensional item set — random assignment, purposeful parceling, the balancing technique — are described by Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) for items that measure one construct. Random assignment in particular is said to rest on the strong assumption that the items are interchangeable in the population, and to be recommended for unidimensional indicators, or for a large set of items with high communality, with sampling variability low; the sample size they attach to that condition is above about 200. When items are not interchangeable, the allocation is no longer arbitrary and the choice of allocation begins to matter.

The consequence of parceling items that are not unidimensional is the main argument against parceling that these authors report, and they attribute it to Bandalos (2002): with multidimensional or multifaceted constructs, parcels can bias the estimated factor loadings in the measurement model and confuse the interpretation of the structural relations. Their own position is that this holds when parcels are created randomly or improperly, and that parcels built by a strategy suited to the dimensionality of the items — the two multidimensional strategies below — can make the structure of a multidimensional construct easier rather than harder to interpret, a position they attribute to Graham, Tatterson and Widaman (2000).

What to do with a multidimensional item set

Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) describe two schemes, and the choice between them is a choice about what the latent construct is to mean. In facet-representative parceling, the items of one facet go into one parcel, so the uniqueness of the facet is isolated inside that parcel and the common factor captures only what the facets share; this is an implicit way of estimating what would otherwise be a higher-order model, and they recommend it when the interest is in the higher-order construct alone. In domain-representative parceling, the items of each facet are spread over the parcels, so the secondary variance becomes common to the parcels and enters the latent factor along with the original common variance; they recommend it when each facet's specific variance is expected to relate to other constructs in the model beyond the variance the facets share, referring that recommendation to Kishton and Widaman (1994) and Little, Rhemtulla, Gibson and Schoemann (2013). They also report that in the comparison of the two schemes on an Internal–External Locus of Control measure by Kishton and Widaman (1994) the domain-representative scheme performed better.

The effect on fit and on parameter estimates

Two effects are separated in the source. The first is that fit improves. Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) report the demonstration in their own Section 3, where an item-level model with poor fit becomes a parcel-level model with good fit, and they attribute a general version of the finding to Landis, Beal and Tesluk (2000): improvements in fit are generally the result of a stronger measurement model. They also note that a model with four parceled indicators per construct rather than three is overidentified and that the extra degrees of freedom typically produce an arbitrary improvement in fit.

The second effect is the argument that parcels camouflage misspecification, which they attribute to Bandalos (2002) and to Marsh, Lüdtke, Nagengast, Morin and von Davier (2013): cross-loadings and residual correlations are harder to detect with parcels, so estimates may be biased, and the better fit may be read as the absence of misspecification. Against this they cite the simulation of Rhemtulla (2016), in which parcel-level models produced similar or less biased estimates more often than item-level models, and in which parcel-level models had lower power to detect a within-factor misspecification or a single cross-loading but higher power to detect multiple cross-loadings and misspecifications in the structural model.

A third effect concerns the allocation itself. Results can vary with the parceling scheme, which Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) list as an argument against parceling and answer in two parts: the parceling decisions should be described so that they can be reproduced, and the variability of the results across allocations of items to parcels can itself be examined and reported, for which they refer to Sterba (2019) and Sterba and MacCallum (2010). Sterba and Rights (2017) is cited by them for the point that a random scheme requires the combinations to be generated and evaluated and a decision rule to be stated for what counts as an optimal set of parcels.

What they recommend reporting

The review of the developmental literature in Section 5 of Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) found that reporting was often incomplete, and states what a report should contain: the results of the preliminary analyses used to inform the strategy, such as an item-level exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis; a rationale for the technique and for the number of parcels; and the items combined in each parcel. Where space does not allow it, they suggest supplementary materials. On checking the parcels against the items, they recommend modeling the data at the item level first, to identify the causes of misspecification and to inform how the parcels are built, attributing that recommendation to Bandalos and Finney (2001), Little, Rhemtulla, Gibson and Schoemann (2013) and Rhemtulla (2016); and they recommend examining local misfit in the parcel-level model, noting that the RMSEA and the SRMR were found by Rhemtulla (2016) to increase in parcel-level models relative to item-level models when there was misspecification.

Two further pieces of guidance from the same source bear on what this page writes. They recommend averaging the items rather than summing them, so that the parcel keeps the metric of the response scale; with effects-coded scaling the mean of the construct is then the mean of the items. And they recommend against proration, that is against averaging the items that are present when some are missing, on the ground that proration is a within-person mean imputation whose assumptions are often untenable and which is associated with bias even under data missing completely at random, referring to Lee, Bartholow, McCarthy, Pedersen and Sher (2015), Mazza, Enders and Ruehlman (2015) and Schafer and Graham (2002). Their suggestion is that the parcel be computed only when all its items are present, and that the incomplete items be entered as auxiliary variables when full information maximum likelihood is used.

How many parcels, and how many items in each

These are the recommendations of Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022), reported as such.

  • Three parcels per construct is the configuration they call optimum. With three indicators the construct is just identified once a scaling constraint is chosen, so there are no within-construct degrees of freedom and all the misfit of the model is between constructs.
  • Four or more parcels leave the construct overidentified. They note that the leftover degrees of freedom typically give an arbitrary improvement in fit, and that overidentified constructs have been known to be awkward under model constraints because the orientation among the loadings can shift.
  • Two parcels are acceptable in longitudinal and multiple-group models, because the constraints that establish measurement invariance identify the loadings and intercepts. In a single-group, single-occasion model a two-indicator construct is underidentified; the parameters can be identified by constraining the two loadings equal, and they report simulation work by Little, Lindenberger and Nesselroade (1999) in which such constraints were on average unbiased.
  • The number of items in a parcel does not have to be the same for every parcel, and not every item has to be assigned to a parcel. An item that sits at the center of the construct — their example is “I feel lonely” in a loneliness scale — can be left as an indicator on its own.
  • They give no minimum number of items per parcel. Their examples run from two items upward, and their remark on designing a measure with parceling in mind is that multiples of three are a good starting point for the number of items, with at least three indicators for each subscale.

Methods and procedures

Each strategy below is the procedure described in the section of Little, Rioux, Odejimi and Stickley (2022) named with it, together with the rules this page uses to settle the cases the description leaves open. Those rules are marked as such.

Reading the item list

One item per line. Where a line contains a tab or a semicolon, that character alone separates the fields, so a comma inside a field is left where it is. Otherwise the line is cut at spaces and then at commas, except in a field that is a decimal number written with a comma, which is kept whole; V1 0,62 and V1,0.62 are therefore both read as an item called V1 with a loading of .62. A header row is used when one of its fields is recognized as a column name, and the columns are then read by name rather than by position. A value that is not a number is reported and no loading is recorded for that item; a loading above one in absolute value is reported, since neither a standardized loading nor a correlation can exceed one.

An Mplus output file is read for its STDYX Standardization section. Windows-1252, UTF-8 and UTF-16 encodings are decoded, Fortran D exponents are accepted, an estimate printed with asterisks in place of its standard error is still read, and the 999.000 sentinel, which Mplus prints for a quantity it could not compute, is discarded rather than read as a loading of 999. Only the first group of a multiple-group output is read, and when the invariance shortcut has written several models into one file the first of them is read. Choosing every factor at once writes the factor name into the facet column, which is the input the two multidimensional strategies need; an item that loads on more than one factor is assigned the factor on which its loading is largest in absolute value, and is named in the message, because such an item is not cleanly a member of one facet.

The balancing technique

Section 3.2.6. The items are put in decreasing order of their loading. The first goes to parcel 1, the second to parcel 2, the third to parcel 3; the fourth then goes back to parcel 3, the fifth to parcel 2, the sixth to parcel 1, and the sequence turns again. With six items and three parcels this is exactly the pairing the source describes, the highest-loading item with the lowest, the second highest with the second lowest, and so on. Rules used here for what the description leaves open: ties are broken by the order in which the items were entered, so the same input always gives the same allocation; an item with no loading is placed after every item that has one, and the page says how many items that applies to; and a negative loading counts as the lowest, which is what the ordering says, with a separate warning that the item should be reverse-coded first.

Random assignment

Section 3.2.1. The items are shuffled by the Fisher–Yates algorithm and then cut into parcels whose sizes are as equal as they can be. The numbers come from a counter-based generator rather than from Math.random, so an allocation is reproduced exactly from its seed; a seed of zero is a valid state. With more than one draw the page repeats the allocation over consecutive seeds, records for each the range of the parcel loading sums, and shows the draw at the median of that distribution, naming the seed that produced it. The median draw is shown rather than the best one because selecting the allocation that looks best is the data-driven step the source asks to be justified. The dispersion reported is the dispersion of the balance of the loadings, which is what can be computed without fitting anything; the parcel-allocation variability of Sterba (2019) and Sterba and MacCallum (2010) is variability in the parameter estimates and the fit, and obtaining it means fitting the model under each allocation.

Purposeful parceling

Sections 3.2.4 and 3.2.5, followed by the balancing technique for the items that were not named, which is the combination described at the end of Section 3.2.6. The groups are supplied by the reader from the modification indices of an item-level model: the source names two patterns worth acting on, a pair of items with a large residual covariance, and a pair of items whose cross-loadings on another construct are of similar size and opposite sign, which cancel when the two are averaged. Rules used here: the largest group is seeded into the first parcel, the next into the second, and so on, with a group beyond the last parcel going to the parcel then holding the fewest items; each remaining item, taken in decreasing order of loading, goes to the parcel with the fewest items, ties broken by the smaller sum of loadings. Because each group occupies a parcel of its own, at most the number of groups plus the number of unnamed items can be filled, and asking for more parcels than that is refused with the number that can be filled.

Facet-representative parceling

Section 3.3.1. Every parcel is built from the items of one facet. When the number of parcels equals the number of facets there is one parcel per facet. When more parcels are asked for, the extra ones are given to the facets with the most items per parcel, and the items of a facet split over two or more parcels are divided by the balancing rule; this follows the source's remark that a facet with more than three scores can itself be parceled. Fewer parcels than facets is refused, because two facets sharing a parcel would defeat the purpose of the scheme, which is to isolate the uniqueness of each facet inside its own parcel. A facet with a single item yields a parcel that is that item, which the source allows and the page reports.

Domain-representative parceling

Section 3.3.2. The items of each facet are spread over the parcels. Rule used here: within a facet the items are taken in decreasing order of loading, and each goes to a parcel that does not yet hold an item of that facet in the current round; among those the parcel with the fewest items is chosen, ties broken by the smaller sum of loadings. A new round begins when every parcel has received one. This guarantees that whenever a facet has at least as many items as there are parcels, every parcel receives at least one item from it. When a facet has fewer items than there are parcels the guarantee cannot hold, and the page names the parcels that lack a facet and the facets they lack, because the parcels are then not interchangeable: they do not sample the same facets.

A priori designs

Section 3.3.3. The allocation is read from the fourth field of each line and nothing is computed. An item whose parcel field is empty, - or 0 is left out of the scheme and named, which is the source's provision that not every item need be assigned. The parcels appear in the order in which their labels first occur. The source's own example of a measure designed this way is the Control, Agency, and Means-Ends Interview, in which each parcel pairs a positively worded item with a reverse-coded negatively worded one, and it warns that an a priori scheme should be checked against the covariance pattern actually observed before it is used.

Balance, and the comparison shown beside it

For each parcel the page reports the number of items, the mean of the loadings of the items in it, and their sum. The mean says how strongly the parcel marks the construct; the sum is what an equal split of total strength would equalize, and is the quantity the balancing technique reduces the spread of. A plain sequential split — the items cut into consecutive blocks in the order entered — is summarized beside the allocation for comparison. Over 2,500 generated problems with four to two hundred items and two to twenty parcels the balancing technique gave a smaller spread of parcel loading sums than that split in 96.8% of them and the same spread in a further 0.3%; the median reduction was 83.6% of the spread the sequential split produced. The page warns when the mean loadings of the parcels range over more than .10; that threshold is a display convention of this page and not a criterion from the literature. It also reports when the parcels differ in size by more than one item, which is not a fault — the source states that parcels need not hold the same number of items — but does mean that a larger parcel is more reliable than a smaller one and will carry a larger loading for that reason alone.

The syntax, and what is checked in it

A parcel written as an average appears as the sum of its items in parentheses divided by their number, rather than through a function that aggregates them, so that the value of the parcel depends on every item in it. The reason is the source's recommendation against proration; the input carries a comment asking that the covariance coverage the program prints be compared with the coverage of the least complete item of each parcel, which is how proration would show itself. Names longer than eight characters, names that are Mplus operators or keywords, names that repeat, and names that are not legal variable names are refused before anything is written, with the offending names given. A parcel name that would collide with an item name is changed and the change is visible in the table. The generated input is then read back and checked against what it was meant to say: that NAMES lists the items as entered, that USEVARIABLES lists the parcels and no item, that there is one DEFINE statement per parcel using exactly the items allocated to it and, for an average, dividing by the right number, that every variable used in DEFINE is declared or created earlier, that the BY statement names every parcel, that no line exceeds the ninety characters the program reads, and that no name exceeds eight characters. The checks are written against the requirement rather than against the generator, so that a generator wrong twice in the same way does not pass.

What the page does not do

It does not fit any model. It cannot tell whether the items in a parcel are unidimensional, cannot compute modification indices, and cannot compare a parcel-level solution with an item-level one; all three have to be done in Mplus, and the source treats the item-level model as the step that comes first. It does not read raw data, so it cannot compute the parcels themselves, only the syntax that computes them, and it cannot check for missing values or for items that are keyed the wrong way round. The loadings it orders items by are whatever is entered: a standardized loading, an item-total correlation, or a number from an exploratory factor analysis, and the page has no way of knowing which. Only one construct is handled at a time; a model with several constructs means running the page once for each and merging the inputs. The two multidimensional strategies take the facet structure as given rather than testing it. Nothing here decides whether a construct should be parceled at all.
